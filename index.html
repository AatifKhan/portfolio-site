Case Study

Dashboards Are Boring.

Enterprise analytics platform redesign · 2024

Situation

A multi-squad enterprise platform had gradually drifted from the organisation’s design system, creating inconsistent UI patterns and increasing delivery friction.

Tension

Teams were improvising UI variants because the design system lacked patterns for complex workflows. Over time, the system became a reference rather than a contract.

Intervention

I audited the product interface, extended missing system patterns, and introduced design-system compliance into the Definition of Ready for engineering stories.

Outcome

The platform regained visual and interaction consistency while the design system evolved into an operational standard used across squads.

Turning a Design System From Reference to Contract

1. Situation

PDM is a multi-year internal enterprise platform used for managing compliance and commit workflows. Over time, as multiple product squads shipped features independently, the interface began drifting away from the organisation’s established design system.

What initially appeared as small tactical deviations had accumulated into systemic inconsistency. Components had diverged, styling overrides had multiplied, and teams had begun creating variations outside the system to meet immediate delivery needs.

This wasn’t just a visual issue. In a platform used for compliance workflows, inconsistent patterns increased cognitive load and the risk of user error. Engineering teams also encountered increasing friction as UI inconsistencies created rework and slowed delivery.

I was brought in as Lead UX/UI Designer to help realign the product with the design system while maintaining delivery across multiple active squads.

2. Tension

The deeper challenge quickly became clear: the problem wasn’t simply interface inconsistency.

The organisation already had a mature design system with established tokens and component libraries. Yet the product had evolved outside those guardrails. New UI variants were created ad hoc, stories entered development without clear component references, and engineers relied on CSS overrides to meet sprint deadlines.

Over time, the design system had quietly shifted from a shared contract into a loose reference library.

A structured audit across more than seventy screens revealed a growing pattern of divergence and several missing patterns required for complex workflows.

The issue was not that teams were ignoring the system—the system simply wasn’t equipped to support the product’s evolving needs.

3. Insight

Rather than enforcing strict compliance with the existing system, the opportunity was to restore alignment between the product and the system that was meant to support it.

Three principles shaped the work:

  • Design system first—reuse or extend existing components before creating bespoke patterns
  • Token discipline—map spacing, colour, typography, and interaction states to system tokens
  • Shared delivery contracts—align design and engineering around component usage before development

The goal was not only to improve the UI, but to reintroduce design-system governance into the delivery pipeline itself.

4. Decision

Working with product managers, engineering leads, and the design-system guild, we introduced design-system compliance directly into the Definition of Ready for product stories.

Every story entering development now required explicit references to approved components and tokens. Decisions were clarified before implementation rather than interpreted during development.

At the same time, the design system was extended to support complex workflows, including multi-stage progress tracking and dense data interactions.

This reframed the design system from a passive reference into an active part of delivery.

5. Execution

The redesign and system alignment were delivered incrementally across multiple releases while product squads continued shipping features.

Key improvements included:

  • Consistent form and validation patterns with clearer error recovery
  • Data-dense table interactions with inline editing and fewer modal workflows
  • Unified navigation and filtering across complex compliance tasks

Recurring design-development pairing and structured reviews for new patterns helped teams move forward without reintroducing fragmentation.

6. Outcome

The platform regained a coherent interface across high-traffic workflows and a more reliable delivery pipeline.

Stories moved through grooming and development with fewer ambiguities, front-end rework decreased, and reliance on CSS overrides was significantly reduced.

Components introduced for the platform were later adopted by other internal products. What had functioned as a reference library began operating as a shared standard across squads.

Reflection

I would introduce lightweight telemetry earlier to better quantify usability improvements and formalise a design-system onboarding playbook for new teams.

The most important outcome was not simply a cleaner interface. It was the restoration of a shared contract between design and engineering.

GeoMarketeer Case study · Draft 02

A promising location.
A lot of unanswered questions.

Helping retail businesses investigate a place before investing in it.

Company
GeoSpoc
Timeline
May–October 2017
My role
UX Researcher & UX/UI Designer

I contributed to research as part of a broader team and owned the end-to-end UX/UI design, from navigation explorations and wireframes through to the final interface.

01 / The idea

Sid wanted a café of his own.

This busy café lane looked like a promising place.

02 / The assumption

The cafés next door were busy.

Surely there was room for one more?

03 / The doubt

But what was bringing people here?

The metro explained some of the crowd. It didn’t tell Sid who would buy.

04 / The context

He needed more than footfall.

Who lives and works nearby? What do they spend? What do the existing cafés already offer?

05 / The response

Same lane. A clearer picture.

GeoMarketeer helps businesses understand a neighborhood before choosing where to open.

01 / 05
Skip introduction

The problem

Business owners and local franchise operators needed to investigate retail locations. They were gathering information manually, while existing GIS tools offered workflows that often assumed specialist knowledge.

The research

Research with six businesses over six weeks revealed difficulties understanding demographics, competition and pricing. The team also observed two businesses more closely for about a month.

My contribution

I contributed to the broader research effort and owned the end-to-end UX/UI. I explored several navigation arrangements and developed the feature structure, wireframes, final interface designs and onboarding.

The design direction

Datasets, Filters, Basemaps and Reports shared a consistent left navigation. Drawing on Google Maps as a reference, detailed controls appeared progressively beside the map, giving owners room to investigate without covering the location.

Launch and continued feedback

GeoMarketeer launched and was used by local business and franchise operators in Pune to investigate retail locations and produce analysis. We continued gathering their feedback on usability and how information could be presented more usefully.

01 / Understanding the problem

Before designing the map,
we went outside.

Choosing a store location comes with several questions disguised as one.

Who spends time here? What do they buy? What might they pay? Who already serves them?

“Where should we open?” turns out to have quite a large extended family.

Working with the broader research team, I helped investigate how existing and new retail businesses approached these decisions. Existing businesses could describe the realities of entering a market. New businesses helped us understand the uncertainty before that commitment.

Business owners and local franchise operators were the primary users. Researchers and GIS specialists observed their use of the tool, while the owners’ needs and feedback guided the experience.

Over six weeks, we spoke with six businesses, including sports brands, cafés and restaurants. We also observed two businesses more closely for about a month.

Four difficulties kept appearing.

Understanding the area took time.
Businesses were conducting demographic research manually.
Competition was difficult to assess.
Established brands and customer loyalty complicated decisions about entering a market.
Pricing lacked sufficient context.
Businesses didn’t always have enough information to judge what customers might be willing to spend.
Location analysis was hard to access.
Useful geographic information wasn’t readily available in a form they could use.

The businesses were already doing research. Much of the work involved gathering scattered information and trying to assemble a useful picture.

02 / Examining the tools

The information existed.
Finding the right button was another adventure.

Geographic Information Systems—GIS—offered ways to investigate these questions.

A map shows where things are. GIS adds information about those places, allowing someone to explore boundaries, businesses and other characteristics of an area.

That was useful territory for GeoMarketeer.

But our evaluation of existing tools revealed another problem: accessing the information could require considerable effort and specialist knowledge.

We looked at the steps involved in investigating a location—from finding the right view to taking the findings away.

Finding controls
Changing the base map was difficult to discover.
Working with information
Applying filters was unintuitive, and users couldn’t always save the filters they had configured.
Defining the area
Area-selection options didn’t always support the investigation someone wanted to conduct.
Taking the findings away
Reports could be overwhelming, while proprietary formats made them difficult to use outside the GIS application.

Someone researching a potential store location had acquired an unexpected side project: learning GIS.

Our challenge became:

How could retail businesses investigate a location without first becoming experts in the tool?

03 / Turning research into structure

Four jobs.
A lot of geography.

The research generated a substantial feature list.

We needed to account for geographic boundaries, datasets, map types, search, custom filters and reporting. Across India, the underlying information included city extents, pin-code boundaries, localities and other geographic layers.

The temptation was obvious: put everything within reach.

The problem was equally obvious: everything would then be within reach.

We grouped the requirements into four areas:

Four groups of product requirements
AreaWhat someone needed to do
DataChoose the information relevant to their investigation.
MapsFind a place and choose how to view it.
QueriesNarrow the information around their requirements.
ReportsTake the findings away for further use.

This grouping connected the research to three design responses:

  • Gathering information manually: bring location information and relevant datasets into one map-based workspace.
  • Working through complex controls: reveal task-specific options and filters progressively.
  • Taking findings away: make downloadable reports part of the core workflow.

That last need came directly from the research: owners wanted reports they could download. The work needed to remain useful after someone closed the browser.

04 / Exploring the navigation

The map had company.
Quite a lot of it.

Our early designs drew from conventional GIS interfaces. Actions sat across toolbars, navigation menus and dialogs over the map. A left sidebar was already part of that arrangement.

We tested these early layouts with business owners and continued gathering feedback as the designs developed. I owned the UX/UI iterations within that broader research effort.

I explored several navigation arrangements, including top and floating toolbars, side panels and split layouts. The sketches helped me consider where the main tasks would live, how much room their controls needed, and how much geographic context remained visible.

The final structure brought Datasets, Filters, Basemaps and Reports into a consistent left navigation. Selecting an activity revealed its detailed controls alongside the map.

This let us accommodate additional options and filters progressively: the opening view could stay focused, with more detail available as owners continued their investigation.

Google Maps informed the relationship between the map and its supporting panel. The trade-off was deliberate: some horizontal map space went to the controls, giving owners room to work with information while keeping the location in view.

Two changes to the workflow

Reading the early and final screens together shows two changes. The concerns below describe the design analysis of those layouts.

A consistent home for the main activities

Early layout
Import, analysis, export and map configuration were distributed across toolbars and navigation.
Design concern
Owners had to understand this arrangement to locate the next relevant activity.
Design response
Group Datasets, Filters, Basemaps and Reports in one clearly labelled navigation, with relevant details beside it.

Keep the location in view while working

Early layout
Several configuration screens opened in dialogs over the map.
Design concern
Those dialogs covered part of the geographic context while the owner worked with settings.
Design response
Reveal detailed controls in an adjacent panel so the map and current task remain visible together.

05 / Refining the experience

A home for the tools.
Room to investigate.

The final interface carried the navigation structure through to the details of the workspace.

The main tasks stayed together.
Datasets, Filters, Basemaps and Reports occupied a consistent location in the left sidebar.
Detailed controls appeared beside the map.
Opening the dataset panel added working space without replacing the geographic view.
Supporting functions had their own place.
Help, settings and account controls sat below the main workflow.
Onboarding introduced the workspace.
The introductory screens highlighted the main tools and explained what each could do.

These details helped express the structure of the product: what was available, where it lived and how to begin.

06 / Bringing the experience together

From “this place looks promising” to a more useful question.

Return to someone like Sid.

The starting point is a location. The next step is deciding what to investigate about it.

The experience was designed to support that progression:

  1. Find an area.

    Locate the place under consideration.

  2. Choose relevant information.

    Select the datasets and layers that help describe it.

  3. Narrow the investigation.

    Apply filters based on the business’s requirements.

  4. Take the findings away.

    Generate a report for use beyond the application.

A map became the workspace for investigating a business question.

07 / Launch and continued feedback

The conversation continued
after launch.

GeoMarketeer launched as a product. Local business and franchise operators in Pune used it to investigate retail locations and produce analysis.

Among them were local operators associated with Starbucks, Nike and Manyavar.

We continued gathering their feedback on what needed improvement and how information could be presented more usefully. Their experience of using the product kept informing discussions about usability and the interface.

My contribution spanned the end-to-end UX/UI, supported by the broader research team: feature structure, several navigation arrangements, wireframes, final interface designs and onboarding.

The work brought a broad set of geographic capabilities into everyday actions: finding an activity, choosing information, adjusting an investigation and taking the findings away.

GeoSpoc was later acquired by Ola. GeoMarketeer was subsequently shelved.

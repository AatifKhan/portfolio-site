Case Study

Dashboards Are Boring.

Enterprise analytics platform redesign · 2024

Situation

A multi-squad enterprise platform had gradually drifted from the organisation’s design system, creating inconsistent UI patterns and increasing delivery friction.

Tension

Teams were improvising UI variants because the design system lacked patterns for complex workflows. Over time, the system became a reference rather than a contract.

Intervention

I audited the product interface, extended missing system patterns, and introduced design-system compliance into the Definition of Ready for engineering stories.

Outcome

The platform regained visual and interaction consistency while the design system evolved into an operational standard used across squads.

Turning a Design System From Reference to Contract

1. Situation

PDM is a multi-year internal enterprise platform used for managing compliance and commit workflows. Over time, as multiple product squads shipped features independently, the interface began drifting away from the organisation’s established design system.

What initially appeared as small tactical deviations had accumulated into systemic inconsistency. Components had diverged, styling overrides had multiplied, and teams had begun creating variations outside the system to meet immediate delivery needs.

This wasn’t just a visual issue. In a platform used for compliance workflows, inconsistent patterns increased cognitive load and the risk of user error. Engineering teams also encountered increasing friction as UI inconsistencies created rework and slowed delivery.

I was brought in as Lead UX/UI Designer to help realign the product with the design system while maintaining delivery across multiple active squads.

2. Tension

The deeper challenge quickly became clear: the problem wasn’t simply interface inconsistency.

The organisation already had a mature design system with established tokens and component libraries. Yet the product had evolved outside those guardrails. New UI variants were created ad hoc, stories entered development without clear component references, and engineers relied on CSS overrides to meet sprint deadlines.

Over time, the design system had quietly shifted from a shared contract into a loose reference library.

A structured audit across more than seventy screens revealed a growing pattern of divergence and several missing patterns required for complex workflows.

The issue was not that teams were ignoring the system—the system simply wasn’t equipped to support the product’s evolving needs.

3. Insight

Rather than enforcing strict compliance with the existing system, the opportunity was to restore alignment between the product and the system that was meant to support it.

Three principles shaped the work:

  • Design system first—reuse or extend existing components before creating bespoke patterns
  • Token discipline—map spacing, colour, typography, and interaction states to system tokens
  • Shared delivery contracts—align design and engineering around component usage before development

The goal was not only to improve the UI, but to reintroduce design-system governance into the delivery pipeline itself.

4. Decision

Working with product managers, engineering leads, and the design-system guild, we introduced design-system compliance directly into the Definition of Ready for product stories.

Every story entering development now required explicit references to approved components and tokens. Decisions were clarified before implementation rather than interpreted during development.

At the same time, the design system was extended to support complex workflows, including multi-stage progress tracking and dense data interactions.

This reframed the design system from a passive reference into an active part of delivery.

5. Execution

The redesign and system alignment were delivered incrementally across multiple releases while product squads continued shipping features.

Key improvements included:

  • Consistent form and validation patterns with clearer error recovery
  • Data-dense table interactions with inline editing and fewer modal workflows
  • Unified navigation and filtering across complex compliance tasks

Recurring design-development pairing and structured reviews for new patterns helped teams move forward without reintroducing fragmentation.

6. Outcome

The platform regained a coherent interface across high-traffic workflows and a more reliable delivery pipeline.

Stories moved through grooming and development with fewer ambiguities, front-end rework decreased, and reliance on CSS overrides was significantly reduced.

Components introduced for the platform were later adopted by other internal products. What had functioned as a reference library began operating as a shared standard across squads.

Reflection

I would introduce lightweight telemetry earlier to better quantify usability improvements and formalise a design-system onboarding playbook for new teams.

The most important outcome was not simply a cleaner interface. It was the restoration of a shared contract between design and engineering.

GeoMarketeer

Designing for decisions, not data · GeoSpoc · 2017

The bet

Two cafés can look equally promising on a map, yet one can thrive while the other closes. Location is more than roads and buildings.

The gap

Retail businesses were researching manually, while GIS tools that held useful answers were often built for specialists.

The question

How might we make geospatial intelligence useful to retail businesses without asking them to become GIS experts?

The approach

We explored a web-based experience around relevant data layers, map customisation, targeted queries, and reports.

01 / A promising corner

Siddharth wants to open a café. This corner looks promising.

There is traffic, a large commercial building, and plenty of movement. From here, it feels like an easy yes.

02 / The unanswered question

Plenty of people pass this shop. How many could become customers?

A busy road proves that people are moving. It does not prove that they want coffee—or can conveniently stop for it.

03 / Nearby customers

Who actually lives and works around here?

Homes and offices may sit close together, yet represent very different routines, budgets, and reasons to visit.

04 / Timing

Are those people nearby when the café needs them?

A location can feel alive at rush hour and strangely empty at lunch. Demand changes throughout the day.

05 / Access

Nearby does not always mean easy to reach.

Entrances, crossings, parking, and the side of the road can turn a short distance into a surprisingly inconvenient trip.

06 / Alternatives

Where do people already get their coffee?

Competition is not automatically bad. It can reveal demand—but it also shows where habits and loyalty already exist.

07 / Another possibility

A few streets away, a quieter storefront becomes interesting.

It attracts less passing traffic, but sits closer to the customers, routines, and access points that matter.

08 / The decision

The city did not change. The quality of the decision did.

Before committing to a place, understand what surrounds it. That is the problem GeoMarketeer set out to make easier.

01/ 08

A perfectly good café in a perfectly terrible location

Imagine two people opening identical cafés. Same coffee. Same prices. Same slightly overpriced croissants. One becomes the neighbourhood favourite; the other closes within six months.

A hundred things could explain the difference. But one variable is difficult to change after opening: location. One café might sit near offices full of people searching for their third coffee of the day. The other might be in a neighbourhood where people prefer making tea at home.

That led to the question behind GeoMarketeer: what if a business could understand a neighbourhood before investing in it - not only its roads and buildings, but the people, spending patterns, lifestyles, and competition around it?

1. The expensive art of guessing

Opening a retail store involves many decisions: what to sell, whom to serve, how much to charge, and where to open. Businesses often answer them through experience, observation, market research, and whatever information they can gather.

During research with six businesses over six weeks, we found that important location decisions were often made with incomplete information. Some teams spent considerable time studying demographics manually. Others struggled to understand the competitive landscape, estimate prices, or access location analysis at all.

These were not businesses that did not care about research. They lacked an accessible way to bring location intelligence into the decisions that mattered.

2. The powerful tool nobody asked to learn

Geographic Information Systems already made it possible to analyse places. An ordinary map tells you where things are; GIS can also describe the people, businesses, and characteristics around those places.

For a retailer, that could help answer useful questions about customers, neighbourhoods, and potential competition. Yet our evaluation of existing GIS tools found crowded toolbars, unfamiliar terminology, dense visualisations, and controls that assumed specialist knowledge.

The technology was powerful. The experience of using it was the barrier. Wanting to find a good location for a coffee shop should not first require learning a geographic analysis workstation.

3. Reframing the problem

The research suggested that two problems were reinforcing one another: businesses lacked access to useful location information, and the tools that could provide it were difficult to learn and operate.

Simply providing more data would not solve everything. We also needed to consider how people would access, understand, and act on that information.

Our design question became: How might we make geospatial intelligence accessible and understandable to retail businesses without requiring specialised GIS expertise?

4. Making sense of complexity

GeoMarketeer needed to work with extensive geographic data across India, including city extents, pin-code boundaries, locality boundaries, and other layers. Showing everything at once would create the same overwhelming experience we were trying to avoid.

We organised the experience around four useful jobs: switching between relevant data layers, customising maps, applying targeted queries, and exporting reports for offline use.

  • Data: make large-scale geographic information easier to explore.
  • Maps: support base maps, custom layers, and search without losing orientation.
  • Queries: help different businesses focus on what matters to them.
  • Reports: let people take their findings into decisions made away from the screen.

5. From exploration to experience

I used research, sketches, wireframes, and early interface explorations to make the product feel less like a specialist workstation and more like a business decision tool.

The final direction brought the map to the centre of the experience. Users could select areas of interest, move through relevant layers, apply filters based on their needs, and generate custom reports.

The aim was not to hide complexity. It was to reveal the right part of it at the right time, in language that made the information feel usable.
